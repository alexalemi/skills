GOALS, OBJECTIVES, AND POLICIES
This section stipulates goals, objectives, and implementing policies for the Recreation and Open Space
Element pursuant to §163.3177(6) (e), Florida Statutes.

GOAL 6.1: PROVIDE ADEQUATE RECREATION AND OPEN SPACE.
Ensure provision of a comprehensive system of recreation facilities and open space sites that meet
the needs of existing and projected user groups within the City.

       OBJECTIVE 6.1.1: SYSTEM OF PARKS AND RECREATION.
       The system of parks and recreation facilities shall continue to be improved and maintained over
       time in order to meet the needs of the existing and projected future population, including
       recreational and open space outside of the City of Kissimmee limits in unincorporated Osceola
       County but proximal to the City.

               POLICY 6.1.1.1: LEVEL OF SERVICE STANDARDS FOR PARKS AND RECREATION FACILITIES.
               The City of Kissimmee shall continue to maintain a minimum level of service standard of
               5 acres per 1000 total residents. This standard shall be used as level of service criteria in
               reviewing proposals for development orders or permits. When possible, the City shall go
               beyond this standard and attain a higher level of service for its parks and recreation facilities.

               POLICY 6.1.1.2: BALANCE OF PARK TYPES.
               The City will continue to provide a balance of recreational facilities and parks to meet the
               recreational needs of its existing and future residents including neighborhood, community,
               regional, and special use park types, as well as large urban parks.

               POLICY 6.1.1.3: DISTRIBUTION OF PARK TYPES.
               The City shall continue to strive to locate new neighborhood, community, and special use
               parks and recreation facilities in areas and neighborhoods with the least access to existing
               parks and recreation facilities, in order to create an equitable distribution of parks throughout
               the City. Access to different park types shall be based on a comparison of the approximate
               travel distances on the transportation network anticipated for each park type to the
               maximum recommended travel distances for each park type. Recreational and open space
               areas should adjust with changing community preferences and may include passive and
               active uses such as urban plazas, place-making mini-parks, cultural facilities, and dog parks
               or skate parks.

DATA, INVENTORY, AND ANALYSIS
INTRODUCTION
This Element presents an inventory of existing recreational facilities and parks within and near the City
that are accessible to the public. The bounty of natural resources in the City and surrounding area
present a wealth of recreation opportunities, and the City has capitalized on that proximity to establish
and maintain a number of parks that showcase these amenities. Principally among these are Shingle
Creek Regional Park and Lakefront Park. Also included in the recreation inventory are other municipal
and county resource-based parks, as well as sites and parks geared toward programmed activities and
organized sports.

EXISTING CITY RECREATION FACILITIES
All existing public-owned recreation facilities within the City are either resource- or activity-based facilities
and are classified according to size and use. In resource-based facilities, recreational activities are based
on the presence of a specific natural resource, such as a lake or stream. Activities available at resource-
based parks tend to be passive in nature, such as picnicking, hiking, boating, and bird watching. Activity-
based facilities rely on the presence of facilities that provide sites for programmed activities and sports,
such as basketball courts or soccer fields, and which may also be used for unstructured play activities. A
Recreation Space Classification System was developed and the different park types within the City are
classified into the categories defined below.

NEIGHBORHOOD PARK
This park type incorporates mini-parks and school
parks, as well as neighborhood parks. A
neighborhood park may be less than an acre in size, or
up to 10 acres. It is usually within a quarter mile to a
half mile distance of users’ homes, uninterrupted by
non-residential uses, wide roads and other physical
barriers, or is located adjacent to school property.
These parks are typically developed in or near
residential areas to provide public parks for specific neighborhoods. Travel to the park is usually
conducted by walking or bicycling. Neighborhood parks are usually operational during daylight hours only
and contain amenities such as open space, playgrounds, practice fields and picnic facilities.

COMMUNITY PARK
This park type serves a broader purpose than a neighborhood park, and the focus is on meeting
community based recreational needs as well as preserving unique landscapes and open spaces. A
community park is typically more than 10 acres in size, and may be up to 50 acres. It usually serves the
parks and recreation needs of multiple neighborhoods and users may travel between a half mile and
three miles to the park. Travel to the park is primarily conducted by walking, bicycling and motor vehicles.
Community parks may typically contain an indoor recreation facility, lighted fields and courts, swimming
pools and parking lots and are staffed and open to the public during evening hours.

SPECIAL USE PARK
These types are unique in nature and cover a broad
range of park and recreation facilities and uses that
are typically oriented towards a single purpose. They
may be located on small parcels of land or may
encompass a hundred acres or more, depending on
the type and intensity of use. Travel to the park would
typically be by motor vehicle. Users may travel longer
distances and from outside the community to utilize a
special use park. A special use park would contain
higher intensity uses such as large public functions of an outdoor or indoor nature. Certain sports
complexes, civic centers, aquatic centers, amphitheaters, and the like may be classified as special use
parks.

LARGE URBAN PARK
This park type serves a broader purpose than community parks, these are used when community and
neighborhood parks are not adequate to serve the needs of the community. The focus is on meeting
community based park and recreational needs on a larger scale. The location of such a park is
determined by the quality and suitability of the site and it usually serves the entire community. The size of
a large urban park may vary but would usually contain a minimum of 20 acres, but up to 100 acres would
not be uncommon. Travel to this type of park would typically be by motor vehicle but depending on its
location, bicycles and pedestrians could also access the park. A large urban park could be open during
evening hours and may be nature oriented or have multiple amenities that serve a particular purpose,
such as boating, camping, or hiking. The only large urban park in the City is the Lakefront Park.

REGIONAL PARK
These parks provide recreational amenities to a geographic area wider than just the City and nearby
areas. Typically larger than 100 acres, these parks serve multi-county areas and may be accessed by all

The recreation and open space inventory above shows that the City of Kissimmee has within its borders
978 acres of publicly-owned property for recreational use, which based on its estimated 2015 population
of 65,422, represents nearly 15 acres per 1,000 population. This total includes school facilities as
neighborhood parks, which are available to City residents and are discussed further below. If these
facilities are not included in the calculations, the City’s total is 973 acres, or approximately 14.9 acres per
1,000 total residents.

An interlocal agreement was established between the School Board and the City in 1988, which has
since been revised several times and described further below, to allow the City to utilize school
recreational areas and facilities for general public use. The schools represent facilities that the City Parks
and Recreation Department currently utilize on an ongoing basis for City-sponsored programs.

RECREATION FACILITIES MAINTAINED BY OTHER ENTITIES
The City is not the only jurisdiction to provide parks and recreational facilities to residents of the City of
Kissimmee. The following entities maintain recreation sites within or near the City that may be used by
City residents. These facilities are not counted toward the achievement of the City’s recreation level of
service standard.

OSCEOLA COUNTY RECREATION FACILITIES
In 1999, the City joined Osceola County, St. Cloud and the Osceola County School Board in recognizing
that each contributes to the overall parks and recreation system within the County. The Cities of St.
Cloud and Kissimmee offer comprehensive parks and recreation programs, and are responsible for
providing a majority of active recreation opportunities to City and County residents, as well as residents
in nearby Orange County. Osceola County, on the other hand, tends to provide mostly passive parks,
boat ramps and special use parks such as the County’s Softball Complex, and many of these are outside
the City’s boundary. The County also has a significant amount of undeveloped park lands that will
eventually be open to the public for active and passive recreational uses. As the area grows and
becomes increasingly urban, the county-wide system is under great pressure to provide additional
program capacity, upgraded facilities and new parks and recreation facilities.

PUBLIC SCHOOL SITE RECREATION FACILITIES
An official agreement exists between the Osceola
County School Board and the City of Kissimmee to
allow the City to use school “property and any
facilities constructed thereon” for recreational
purposes, including playgrounds and athletic
facilities at area schools. The Master Joint Use and
Development of Recreational Facilities agreement
was signed in May 1988, revised in 1997, 2003, and
most recently in 2008, and outlines use and
maintenance responsibilities. Co-location and the
joint-use of facilities was also incorporated into the
2003 interlocal agreement for the coordination of public school facilities planning, and the 2009
agreement relating to school concurrency and the planning and coordination of public school facilities.
School facilities are only available when the property is not being used for school purposes, however.

STATE AND FEDERAL RECREATION FACILITIES
Prairie Lake State Preserve, Three Lakes Wildlife Management Area, and Bull Creek Wildlife
Management Area are managed by the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission. They are all
located in close proximity to each other, and approximately 8 miles north of Kenansville on SR 523. The
three parks provide picnic areas, a boat ramp, hunting and fishing areas and hiking and biking trails.

PRIVATE RECREATION FACILITIES
A vast number of private recreational facilities within or near Kissimmee are available to the public. The
City is within a twenty mile drive of several large, nationally recognized recreation attractions and
amusement parks including Disney World Resort, Sea World of Orlando, and Universal Studios. A
number of smaller commercial recreational facilities are also located within or adjacent to Kissimmee,
including golf courses, batting cages, and putt-putt golf courses.

Additionally, many private single family and multi-family housing developments provide much of the
recreational opportunities for the City’s residents. Facilities include swimming pools, tot lots, tennis
courts, open spaces, trails and the like.

CURRENT RECREATION DEMANDS AND NEEDS
The quality of recreation cannot be judged solely on the amount of land area dedicated to and developed
for recreational uses, but invariably must consider the facilities provided, as well as the desires of the
community. In general, facilities located in City parks enable residents to participate in both active and

The City of Kissimmee has determined that parks should be designed to provide service to both active

Rather than establish a level of service (LOS) for each park type, the City has adopted an overall per
capita LOS of five acres per 1,000 population. Based on this minimum standard, the City’s estimated

demands. The City currently has a total of 972.66 acres of non-school recreation facilities, which is over
600 acres more than the population would demand, based on the five acre per 1,000 standard. With the
projected population of 92,445 in 2045, the City will still meet the minimum standard with more than ten
acres per 1,000 people.

provide an opportunity for co-location of recreation facilities for nearby residents. This area is also served
by the Kissimmee Trail that provides nearby residents access to schools and nearby Shingle Creek.

Special Use parks are in high demand and provide additional opportunities to provide residents with
needed active recreational facilities such as pools, ball fields and football/soccer fields. By involving the

are attracted to the area by large amusement and

Although the City (when all acreage of the Shingle Creek Regional Park is included) has a large surplus

distribution of recreation facilities, and to keep pace with future recreational space and facility demands.

Extension and development of new and existing trails, bikeways and sidewalks will increase access
between neighborhoods and public spaces, which effectively increases the service area of smaller park
types. These parks and facilities should be designed to serve those specific areas and provide facilities
according to the needs of residents of the surrounding areas.

The least expensive and most expedient means of augmenting recreation services include:

2. Upgrading and improving open spaces to serve a more intensive recreational function where
   consistent with conservation objectives.

USE OF SCHOOL RECREATION SITES
The use of school recreation sites for public recreation purposes represents an efficient use of these
facilities and of public tax expenditures. Once students leave school grounds after daily classes end,
recreational facilities are left unused when not made available to the public. School recreation facilities
are available for public use through the Interlocal Agreements between the City, the County, the City of
St. Cloud, and the Osceola County School Board, which specifically include a section on co-location of
public schools and public recreation facilities. At times, however, these existing school facilities must be
augmented and improved before they will adequately meet recreational needs of the general public.

The City of Kissimmee and the School District will continue to coordinate all future development plans so
decisions on future school site locations can incorporate both educational and recreational needs of the
City’s residents. Similarly, the locations of parks near schools also compliment the athletic and recreation
activities available to students. Since school recreation sites will generally serve areas identical to their
education service areas, school facilities should be utilized for the following recreational purposes:

   Elementary School facilities as Neighborhood Parks

   Middle School facilities as Neighborhood or Community Parks

   High School facilities as Neighborhood, Community or Special Use Parks

Recreational facilities located within these school types should comply with Department of Education
guidelines, and the City shall coordinate with the School District to ensure the facilities will also meet City
standards for the above recreational classification.

COORDINATION WITH CONSERVATION AREAS AND NATURAL RESOURCES
In addition to their recreational purposes, parks located between natural resource protection areas and
urban development often serve to protect the health and safety of residents by preventing natural or
man-induced hazards from entering populated areas. As open space, parks can serve as buffers
between incompatible land uses, such as industrial and residential uses. Their presence can also
prevent development from intruding into, and detrimentally affecting, environmentally sensitive areas,
such as wetlands and certain vegetative communities. Future recreational spaces may best be located
along or within natural resource areas, where they can serve both recreational and conservation
functions. A number of Kissimmee’s parks are already located adjacent to natural resources.

Natural waterways and canals, and stormwater management areas, represent ideal locations to develop
linear parks and open space corridors, as well as pedestrian and bicycle pathways that can enhance and
diversify the City’s transportation network. With park locations already bordering these corridors, the City
can continue to develop an inter-connected park system that traverses through several residential
neighborhoods.

DISTRIBUTION OF FUTURE RECREATIONAL FACILITIES
Recreational space, particularly neighborhood and community parks, should be located in areas
accessible to Kissimmee’s population. Current recreational space may meet the adopted LOS for total
area, but existing parks may not necessarily be located in areas of current and future residential growth.
Many parks are located south of Vine Street, but new development is most likely to the northwest and
northeast portions of the City. Additionally, redevelopment opportunities are primarily located in the
central area of the City. Future recreational spaces will need to be located in areas where growth and
development are anticipated to occur.

The City of Kissimmee should continue to coordinate its future recreation plans with the County and
School District to avoid duplication of services provided by both entities. The City of Kissimmee should
also encourage the County to implement the proposed parks and recreation system outlined in the
County Master Plan, which is in the process of being updated for the new decade. The City will also
continue to require private developers to supply parks and recreational facilities as part of development
projects with residential uses, according to the Land Development Code.

SUMMARY
The City will continue to provide parks and recreational facilities to existing and projected residents, and will
do so by taking into account residents’ recreational preferences, geographic concentrations, and land
available for recreation sites. In order to provide additional facilities, the City may require the dedication of
public recreation facilities as part of large private development projects. Additionally, as the City annexes
land, recreation facilities may come to be located within the City boundary. This will increase the quantity
of recreation lands with the City, and raise the actual LOS above the minimum required standard.
