# Strong Towns Principles for Planning Advisory Board Review

This guide provides an overview of Strong Towns principles and how they can be applied as an advisory framework when evaluating development applications.

## Disclaimer

**This is an advisory framework, not a requirement.** Strong Towns principles provide additional considerations for evaluating the long-term financial sustainability and community impact of development proposals. They supplement, but do not replace, comprehensive plan policies, land development code requirements, and Florida Statutes.

---

## What is Strong Towns?

**Strong Towns** is a 501(c)3 non-profit organization founded by Charles Marohn, a professional engineer and land use planner. The organization advocates for financially sustainable, resilient urban development as an alternative to post-World War II suburban sprawl.

**Core Philosophy:**
Modern suburban development operates like a "Growth Ponzi Scheme"—cities receive immediate revenue from new development but accumulate massive long-term infrastructure maintenance liabilities they cannot afford. The solution is a return to traditional development patterns: incremental, human-scaled, financially productive, and adaptable growth.

**Key Books:**
- "Strong Towns: A Bottom-Up Revolution to Rebuild American Prosperity" (2019)
- "Confessions of a Recovering Engineer: Transportation for a Strong Town" (2021)

**Website:** www.strongtowns.org

## The Growth Ponzi Scheme

### How It Works

The suburban development pattern operates through three financing mechanisms:
1. **Transfer payments** from federal/state governments
2. **Transportation spending** enabling greenfield site development
3. **Public and private debt** financing

### The Problem

Cities receive immediate benefits (permit fees, utility charges, tax revenue) but assume decades-long maintenance obligations. Over a development's lifecycle, cities often receive only **10-20 cents in revenue for every dollar of liability** they incur.

###The Ponzi Dynamic

Like any pyramid scheme, cities try to overcome insolvency by growing faster. New development temporarily alleviates budget pain but increases future obligations, creating a ticking time bomb of deferred maintenance estimated in the trillions of dollars nationwide.

### Implications for PAB

Every new development represents a long-term financial commitment. Consider: **Will this development generate sufficient tax revenue to maintain its infrastructure perpetually?** Low-density, auto-dependent sprawl almost never does.

## The Six Principles for Building Strong Towns

### 1. Financial Solvency

**Cities must be solvent.** Every development should be evaluated by calculating both short-term costs and long-term maintenance expenses. Streets are liabilities, not assets. Value-per-acre analysis reveals which development patterns actually generate tax revenue sufficient to maintain infrastructure.

### 2. Land as Base Resource

**Land is the finite resource** from which community prosperity is built. The activity on land must create enough wealth to support necessary infrastructure. Productive land use determines whether communities can sustain themselves economically.

### 3. Transportation as Means, Not End

**Transportation systems must generate prosperity**, not serve as ends unto themselves. Example: Kansas City added 1,600 miles of streets for only 13% population growth, creating unsustainable maintenance obligations. Every transportation investment must build lasting economic health.

### 4. Economic Development as "Gardening"

**Job creation results from healthy local economies**, not vice versa. Cities shouldn't race to the bottom with tax breaks chasing major employers. Instead, develop strong fundamentals that naturally attract businesses—more like gardening than big-game hunting.

### 5. Strong Citizens

**Places endure when residents assume ownership.** Shift from "Residents as Customers" to "Residents as Shareholders." Cities must be created by everybody to provide something for everybody.

### 6. Local Government as Platform

**Local government should be a platform** for citizens to collaboratively build prosperous places, not implement distant policies. Focus on serving people and fostering community co-creation.

## Traditional vs. Suburban Development Patterns

### Traditional Development Pattern (Pre-1940s)

**Characteristics:**
- Human-scaled design (1-3 story buildings)
- Walkable distances (daily needs within walking distance)
- Mixed-use by default (shop on first floor, apartment above)
- Incremental growth over time (not built all at once)
- Compact street grids with short blocks (300-650 feet)
- Connected street networks
- Financially productive ($1M+ per acre common)

**Benefits:**
- Tremendous financial upside, limited downside
- Resilient and adaptable to change
- Spreads risk across many small investors
- Creates local wealth-building opportunities
- Minimal infrastructure cost per capita

### Suburban Development Pattern (Post-1940s)

**Characteristics:**
- Built all at once to a "finished state"
- Auto-dependent design
- Single-use zoning (separated uses)
- Large-scale projects financed by debt
- Long blocks (1,500+ feet) and disconnected streets
- Cul-de-sacs and hierarchical street networks
- Low financial productivity (often <$200K per acre)

**Problems:**
- Incredibly fragile financially
- Limited upside, massive downside risk
- Unmanageable debt and liability
- Susceptible to economic disruption
- High infrastructure costs per capita

## Key Metrics and Evaluation Criteria

### Value Per Acre (Primary Financial Metric)

**What it measures:** Tax revenue generated per acre of land

**Why it matters:** Normalizes revenues for apples-to-apples comparisons

**Typical findings:**
- Traditional dense mixed-use: $630K - $19M per acre
- Big box retail: <$200K per acre
- Single-family residential: $50K - $150K per acre

**Application:** Compare proposed development's potential value-per-acre against existing development to assess financial productivity.

### Financial Productivity Test

**Questions to ask:**
- What is the lifecycle cost of infrastructure serving this development?
- What annual tax revenue will it generate?
- Does revenue cover maintenance costs in perpetuity?
- What is the revenue-to-liability ratio?

### Connectivity Index

**What it measures:** Street network connectivity
**Standard:** Minimum 1.4 connectivity score
**Calculation:** Links divided by nodes in street network
**Why it matters:** Connected networks reduce service costs (17% more efficient) and enable walkability

### Block Size

**Traditional standard:** 300-650 feet
**Modern problem:** Often 1,500+ feet
**Impact:** Longer blocks increase driving, reduce walkability, encourage speeding

## Streets vs. Roads vs. Stroads

### Streets (Wealth-Creating Places)

**Purpose:** Platform for building wealth through business and residential activity

**Design characteristics:**
- Low speeds (10-25 mph)
- Wide sidewalks
- On-street parking
- Buildings close to street
- Pedestrian-oriented

**Function:** Destination where people interact with businesses and residences

### Roads (Efficient Connectors)

**Purpose:** High-speed connection between productive places

**Design characteristics:**
- Higher speeds (45-65 mph)
- Limited access points
- Grade-separated when possible
- No pedestrian activity

**Function:** Move traffic efficiently between destinations

### Stroads (Dangerous, Unproductive Hybrids) ⚠️

**What they are:** Failed combination of street and road

**Problems:**
- Very dangerous (mix high speeds with pedestrian activity)
- Enormously expensive to build
- Financially unproductive
- Fail at both street and road functions

**Solution:** Convert to either proper street OR proper road—cannot be both

**For PAB:** Identify stroad designs and require commitment to either street or road function, never both.

## Parking Policies

### Core Position

**Eliminate minimum parking requirements** citywide for all uses.

### Problems with Parking Mandates

- Raise housing costs by tens of thousands of dollars
- Write automobile dependence into law
- Make cities hostile to non-drivers
- Cost $5,000 - $50,000 per space to build
- Consume valuable land that could house people or businesses
- Make destinations farther apart
- Often result in oversupply (see #BlackFridayParking campaign)

### Better Approaches

- Allow shared parking in efficient locations
- Let market determine parking supply
- Permit rear or alley parking instead of street-facing lots
- Use on-street parking to calm traffic

### For PAB Review

**Questions to ask:**
- Does the application include excessive parking?
- Could parking be shared or reduced?
- Is parking located behind/beside buildings rather than street-facing?
- Does parking consume land that could be more productive?

**Advisory position:** Generally favor parking reductions and rear/side parking placement.

## Mixed-Use Development

### Core Position

Mixed-use is essential to town building—the standard for thousands of years.

### Benefits

- Enables walkable neighborhoods with nearby services
- Allows buildings to adapt as needs change
- More financially productive than single-use
- Provides entrepreneurs with location flexibility
- Creates "15-minute neighborhoods"

### Regulatory Barriers to Identify

- Use-based zoning that separates residential and commercial
- Prohibitions on home-based businesses
- Lack of allowance for accessory commercial units

### For PAB Review

**Questions to ask:**
- Does the proposal enable mixed uses vertically (shop below, residence above)?
- Does it allow horizontal mixing of uses?
- Will it create walkable destinations?
- Does it enable adaptive reuse over time?

**Advisory position:** Generally support mixed-use proposals and recommend removing barriers.

---

## Incremental Development

### Core Position

**Small investments over a broad area over a long period of time.**

### Characteristics

- Built by homeowners, local builders, neighborhood developers
- Fast and adaptable
- Rooted in community
- Spreads risk across many small bets
- Allows neighborhoods to mature naturally

### Modern Barriers

- Zoning requiring variances for anything beyond single-family
- Complex approval processes favoring large developers
- High regulatory costs prohibitive for small projects
- Minimum lot sizes and setback requirements

### For PAB Review

**Questions to ask:**
- Is this a small-scale, incremental project?
- Does it fit the existing neighborhood pattern?
- Will it enable further incremental development?
- Or is it a large-scale, all-at-once mega-project?

**Advisory position:** Favor incremental development; question mega-projects that represent irreversible large gambles.

## Missing Middle Housing

### What It Is

Housing types between single-family homes and large apartment buildings:
- Duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes
- Cottage courts/bungalow courts
- Courtyard apartments (2-3 stories)
- Townhouses and rowhouses
- Small apartment buildings (4-16 units)
- Live-work units

### Why It's Missing

Literally illegal in most places due to:
- Single-family exclusive zoning
- Minimum lot sizes
- Parking requirements
- Density restrictions
- Complex approval processes

### Affordability Approach

Strong Towns advocates market-based solutions:
- Allow incremental development everywhere
- Enable many hands to create housing in many forms
- Remove regulatory barriers
- Support naturally occurring affordable housing (NOAH)

### For PAB Review

**Questions to ask:**
- Does the proposal include missing middle housing types?
- Will it add housing supply in opportunity-rich areas?
- Does it allow for naturally affordable options?
- Is it small-scale and neighborhood-compatible?

**Advisory position:** Support missing middle housing types and recommend reducing barriers to their development.

## Street Design and Connectivity

### Key Principles

- Design determines behavior (not enforcement or education)
- Slow speeds are critical for safety
- Streets should be "forgiving of human error"
- Choose: street OR road (never stroad)
- Infrastructure for walking/biking is "a rounding error" compared to roads

### Design Elements

- Maximum block lengths (ideally 300-650 feet)
- Connected street networks (minimum 1.4 connectivity index)
- Restrict or prohibit cul-de-sacs (limit to 150 feet if allowed)
- Build-to lines instead of deep setbacks
- Narrow street widths appropriate to function
- Complete networks with street, trail, and sidewalk connections

### For PAB Review

**Questions to ask:**
- Does the development create a connected street network?
- What are the block lengths?
- Are streets designed as streets or as stroads?
- Is the design human-scaled and walkable?
- Does it provide safe pedestrian and bicycle infrastructure?

**Advisory position:** Require connected networks, short blocks, and human-scaled street design. Reject stroad designs.

---

## Building Form and Scale

### Core Position

Regulate form, not use. Focus on building-to-street relationship.

### What Matters

- How buildings relate to public spaces
- Scale matching neighborhood context (typically 1-3 stories)
- Human-scaled proportions ("under the tree line")
- Buildings close to street with parking behind
- Active ground floors
- Enclosure creating "outdoor rooms"

### What Doesn't Matter As Much

- Specific architectural style
- Exact use (coffee shop vs. law office)
- Arbitrary density calculations

### For PAB Review

**Questions to ask:**
- Is the building form compatible with surrounding context?
- Does it maintain human scale?
- How does it relate to the street?
- Is it an incremental step or a giant leap?
- Does the form support future adaptability?

**Advisory position:** Focus on appropriate form and scale rather than specific uses or arbitrary metrics.

## Subdivision Design

### Critical Issues

Subdivision regulations govern land division and shape entire neighborhoods, yet often operate "in complete obscurity."

### Key Problems with Modern Regulations

- Long blocks (1,500+ feet vs. traditional 300-650 feet)
- Disconnected networks and cul-de-sacs
- Dead-end streets (30% more expensive to maintain)
- Auto-centric design from 1970s still in effect

### Recommended Standards

- **Maximum block lengths** enabling walkability
- **Connectivity requirements** with minimum 1.4 index
- **Restrict dead-ends** - prohibit cul-de-sacs where possible; 150 feet max if allowed
- **Traditional lot patterns** allowing incremental development
- **Alley systems** for parking and utilities
- **Street, trail, and sidewalk connections** throughout

### For PAB Review

**Questions to ask:**
- What is the street network connectivity?
- What are block sizes and lengths?
- Are there cul-de-sacs or dead-ends?
- Does it connect to surrounding neighborhoods?
- Will future connectivity be possible?

**Advisory position:** Require connected networks, short blocks, no cul-de-sacs. Connected networks are 17% more efficient for service delivery.

## The Six Zoning Reforms Every Municipality Should Adopt

These reforms remove barriers to productive development:

### 1. Eliminate Minimum Parking Requirements—Citywide, All Uses

Most important reform. Parking mandates raise costs, enforce auto-dependence, consume productive land.

### 2. Legalize Accessory Dwelling Units (ADUs)—And Do It Right

- Approve quickly
- Exempt from impact fees
- Allow detached units
- No parking requirements
- Skip owner-occupancy mandates

### 3. Reduce or Eliminate Minimum Lot and Unit Size Mandates

These exclude people, raise costs, enable segregation. Example: Houston's reduction from 5,000 to 1,400 sf sparked 100,000 new townhomes.

### 4. Legalize Home-Based Businesses

Focus on measurable impacts, not use categories. Exempt no-impact home businesses from permits.

### 5. Expand Ministerial Approvals

- Write objective standards
- 60-90 day permit shot clocks
- "Deemed approved" for unanswered applications
- Written findings required for all denials

### 6. Legalize Multifamily Housing in Commercial Zones

Enables mixed-use redevelopment of underutilized strip malls and office parks.

## Application to PAB Review

### For Rezoning Applications

**Financial Productivity Questions:**
- Will this rezoning enable more financially productive development?
- Does it allow value-per-acre to increase?
- Will it generate sufficient tax revenue to cover long-term infrastructure costs?
- Does it enable incremental development or require mega-projects?

**Development Pattern Questions:**
- Does it move toward traditional patterns or suburban sprawl?
- Will it enable mixed-use development?
- Does it allow missing middle housing?
- Will it create walkable, human-scaled places?

**Advisory Recommendation:**
Generally favor rezonings that enable higher value-per-acre development, allow mixed uses, permit missing middle housing, reduce parking requirements, and enable incremental development.

### For Comprehensive Plan Amendments

**Key Questions:**
- Does this amendment enable financially sustainable development patterns?
- Will it allow land to be used more productively?
- Does it support incremental growth vs. all-at-once development?
- Will it create adaptable, resilient places?

**Advisory Recommendation:**
Support amendments that connect land use with financial sustainability, enable traditional development patterns, allow incremental evolution, and remove barriers to productive land use.

### For Site Plans

**Street and Connectivity:**
- Is street design appropriate (street vs. road, never stroad)?
- What are block lengths and sizes?
- Does it connect to surrounding street network?

**Parking:**
- Is parking excessive or minimized?
- Is parking located behind/beside buildings?
- Could parking be shared or reduced?

**Building Form:**
- How do buildings relate to streets?
- Is scale appropriate (typically 1-3 stories)?
- Active ground floors?

**Land Use:**
- Does it enable mixed uses?
- Will it create walkable destinations?
- Can buildings adapt over time?

**Advisory Recommendation:**
Support site plans with connected streets, minimal parking, appropriate building scale, and mixed-use potential.

### For Variances

**Strong Towns Perspective:**
The need for variances often indicates problematic zoning code, not problematic development. If many reasonable projects require variances, the code is the problem.

**Questions to Ask:**
- Is this variance needed because of arbitrary restrictions?
- Would the project without the variance produce a better outcome?
- Does the underlying regulation serve a valid public interest?

**Common Legitimate Variances:**
- Setback reductions to enable infill
- Parking reductions
- Lot size reductions for missing middle housing
- Height increases for human-scaled buildings (2-3 stories)

**Advisory Recommendation:**
Generally support variances that enable traditional development patterns, reduce parking, allow missing middle housing, and support incremental development. Question variances that enable auto-oriented sprawl.

### For Subdivision Plats

**Street Network (Most Critical):**
- Maximum block lengths? (Target: 300-650 feet)
- Connectivity index? (Minimum: 1.4)
- Any cul-de-sacs or dead-ends? (Prohibit or limit to 150 feet)
- Connections to adjacent areas?

**Advisory Recommendation:**
Require block lengths maximum 650 feet, connectivity index minimum 1.4, prohibit or severely limit cul-de-sacs, require connections to adjacent land, and provide alley systems where appropriate.

## Evaluation Framework: The "Strong Towns Questions"

When reviewing any application, consider these advisory questions:

### Financial Sustainability
1. What is the long-term financial productivity of this development?
2. Will it generate revenue to cover its infrastructure costs perpetually?
3. What is the value-per-acre compared to existing development?

### Development Pattern
4. Is this traditional (incremental, mixed-use, walkable) or suburban (auto-dependent, single-use, all-at-once)?
5. Does it enable or prevent future incremental development?
6. Is it a small bet or a large gamble?

### Human-Scaled Design
7. Is this designed for people or for cars?
8. What is the building scale and street relationship?
9. Will this create a place or just a project?

### Regulatory Approach
10. Does this require many variances due to bad regulations?
11. Are we regulating the right things (form) or wrong things (use)?
12. Does this enable or restrict productive land use?

## When to Push Back (Red Flags)

From a Strong Towns perspective, be cautious of:

- Large-scale, all-at-once mega-projects
- Auto-oriented development with excessive parking
- Single-use zoning preventing adaptation
- Disconnected street networks with cul-de-sacs
- Stroad design (trying to be both street and road)
- Low-density development with high infrastructure costs
- Developments requiring long-term subsidies
- Regulations preventing incremental development

---

## When to Support (Green Lights)

From a Strong Towns perspective, favor:

- Incremental, small-scale development
- Mixed-use enabling walkability
- Missing middle housing types
- Connected street networks with short blocks
- Human-scaled buildings (1-3 stories typically)
- Parking reduced or located behind buildings
- Form-based standards allowing adaptive reuse
- Development by-right without excessive process
- Financial productivity matching infrastructure costs

## Key Talking Points for Board Members

### On Financial Sustainability
"We need to consider the long-term costs this development creates, not just the short-term revenue. Will the tax revenue from this development cover the perpetual costs of maintaining its infrastructure?"

### On Development Pattern
"The traditional development pattern that built our downtown and older neighborhoods has proven financially sustainable for over a century. Why should we approve something different that hasn't proven sustainable?"

### On Transportation
"This design tries to be both a street and a road—a 'stroad'—which makes it dangerous and financially unproductive. It needs to commit to being one or the other."

### On Parking
"This parking requirement forces the developer to consume valuable land that could house people or businesses. Can we reduce or eliminate this requirement?"

### On Incremental Development
"Strong communities are built through many small bets, not a few large gambles. This project is appropriately scaled for incremental development that limits risk."

### On Variances
"The fact that this reasonable project requires so many variances suggests our zoning code is the problem, not this development."

## Summary: Core Strong Towns Principles for PAB

**The Big Ideas:**
1. **Financial sustainability matters** - Development must pay for itself perpetually
2. **Traditional patterns work** - Pre-WWII development is financially productive; post-WWII sprawl is not
3. **Incremental beats transformative** - Many small bets create resilience; large gambles create fragility
4. **Design determines behavior** - Street design, not enforcement, creates safety
5. **Form matters more than use** - How buildings relate to streets matters more than what's inside
6. **Citizens are shareholders** - Not customers of government services
7. **Remove barriers** - Most regulations prevent good development
8. **Value per acre** - The critical metric for financial productivity
9. **Connected beats hierarchical** - Street networks should connect, not isolate
10. **Human-scaled wins** - 1-3 story buildings create productive, beloved places

**The Bottom Line:**
Strong Towns provides a framework for evaluating development through the lens of long-term financial sustainability, traditional development patterns, and human-scaled design. For Planning Advisory Boards, this means consistently asking: **Will this development create a financially sustainable, adaptable, human-scaled place—or another liability in our Growth Ponzi Scheme?**

**Note:** This guide presents Strong Towns principles as an advisory framework to supplement—not replace—comprehensive plan policies, land development code requirements, and legal mandates. Use these principles to inform analysis and recommendations, recognizing that final decisions must be based on applicable law and policy.
